building a professional wardrobe

An app that helps adults entering or re-entering the workforce build a professional wardrobe: see what their workplace expects, shop within a budget, and find clothes that fit.

Project
Semester project, UX/UI Design, Boston University
My role
Solo: research and design
Timeline
Fall 2026, in progress
Tools
Figma, FigJam, user interviews

the app, sketched

Hand-drawn wireframes of the main screens. The high-fidelity prototype in Figma is in progress.

01

The problem

Most businesses want their employees to dress well and professionally. But for someone starting their first job, or coming back to work after time away, it isn’t always clear what that means or how to get there. I focused on young adults and first-time workers.

Research questionHow do adults entering (or re-entering) the workforce manage the process of acquiring a professional wardrobe?
02

Research

I interviewed three people who had started or returned to jobs that needed a professional wardrobe. I asked each of them six questions:

  1. Which jobs needed a professional wardrobe, and what did you wear before?
  2. When did you realize you needed new clothes, and where did you go for guidance?
  3. Where do you buy work clothes, and how do you weigh the trade-offs?
  4. Which pieces do you buy first, and why?
  5. How do you decide when to add, rotate or replace pieces?
  6. How do you feel about the process overall?

  • Interview 1

    21, male

    Internships and co-ops

    • Moved from remote to in-person internships, and from business casual toward business formal
    • Starts thinking about clothes after the job offer, checking the office’s social media and asking his parents
    • Buys more once he sees what coworkers wear and hears the dress code at orientation
    • Shops department stores like Macy’s for quality pieces on sale, and buys shirts before jackets because he wears them more
    • Enjoys it, depending on the workplace, but finds men’s clothes expensive

  • Interview 2

    60, female

    Office jobs, after retail

    • Needed a professional wardrobe for her first office job, with few women in the office to look to as examples
    • Rebuilt her wardrobe when remote work during COVID ended, drawing on fashion content she’d watched
    • Used department stores, catalogs and specialty stores; now buys mostly online, which she finds much easier
    • Prioritizes tops, jackets and a well-fitting suit; pants long enough for her are her “nemesis”
    • Replaces pieces when her size changes or they start to look dated

  • Interview 3

    21, female

    Gym concierge, after retail

    • Realized she’d need new clothes while applying, after a retail job where she wore denim
    • Asked her mom and used Pinterest and TikTok for inspiration
    • Shops mostly secondhand, at thrift stores and on Depop, plus H&M, Aritzia, Banana Republic and TJ Maxx
    • Buys tops first and re-wears the same pants; well-fitting pants are much harder to find than tops
    • Finds shopping “super fun” but wishes the clothes weren’t so expensive
03

What I learned

Patterns

  • Planning starts with the job offer, but most of the shopping happens after people see what coworkers wear or hear the dress code at orientation.
  • Tops come first, and pants stay basic.
  • People shop where they can balance quality and price.

Surprises

  • Pants were a big pain point, even though they were the lowest priority.
  • Most people weren’t worried about having the right clothes on day one. They were comfortable building up pieces over time.
Key insightAdults entering the workforce build their professional wardrobe piece by piece, after the job is secured.

That pointed to an opportunity: helping people see what’s expected in a specific role or workplace before their first day. It also left me with new questions. Does building a wardrobe piece by piece cause problems of its own? How do people spot the gaps in their wardrobe? And what about clothes for interviews and recruiting?

04

Who I designed for

I brought what I heard together into one persona, Maya. She’s just started her first in-person corporate job, and she worries about cost the way the people I interviewed did.

M

Maya

22 · Boston · Executive assistant (corporate)

Budget-savvy young professional

Maya just started her first in-person corporate job after a more casual retail and college background. She recently graduated with a bachelor’s degree and lives alone. So she wouldn’t buy clothes she didn’t need, she didn’t think much about her wardrobe until she was hired. Now she’s working out her office’s dress code by watching coworkers and scrolling social media.

05

User stories

From the research, I wrote three opportunities, a user story for each, and a flow for how Maya would move through the app. Together they cover the three things my interviewees struggled with: knowing what to wear, paying for it, and finding pieces that fit.

01 · Know the dress code

As a new hire starting my first corporate job, I want to see what people in my role and workplace actually wear, so that I can buy the right clothes before my first day.

Problem
New professionals don’t know what a workplace expects them to wear until after they’re hired or have started, so they buy clothes without knowing the dress code.
Why it matters
Guessing wrong wastes money, risks a bad first impression, and reflects poorly on the company.
Scenario
Maya accepts an executive assistant offer and starts in two weeks. She realizes she doesn’t know what counts as “professional” there.

Steps

  1. Enter her job role and company The app shows a dress-code summary and example outfits for that role.
  2. Browse outfits from coworkers, employer guidelines or similar workplaces Examples are grouped by formality and personal style.
  3. Save outfits she likes They go to a saved board to look at later.
  4. Add her start date She gets suggestions for what to buy first and what can wait until she starts.

Flow

  1. Opens the app after a job offer
  2. Enters role, company and start date

  3. Is there company-specific info?

    Yes Shows the employer’s dress code and examples
    No Shows examples from similar roles, and offers to ask a coworker or the hiring contact
  4. Browses and saves looks
  5. A saved board of outfit ideas and a “buy now / wait” list

02 · Stay on budget

As a new professional on a budget, I want to compare work-appropriate options across new, sale and secondhand sources in one place, so that I can build a polished wardrobe without overspending.

Problem
New professionals struggle to balance quality and affordability when building a work wardrobe.
Why it matters
Without help, people spend more than they can afford or buy pieces that wear out quickly.
Scenario
Maya has two weeks until her start date and a set budget. She wants a few key pieces, but doesn’t want to, or have time to, check five different stores.

Steps

  1. Set a budget and choose item types The app filters to work-appropriate options within budget.
  2. Filter by source Results update with prices and sales.
  3. Compare items by price and quality (brand, material, reviews) A side-by-side view gives each item a score, so the best value is easy to spot.
  4. Add items to her wardrobe board A running total shows her spending against her budget.

Flow

  1. Opens the app with a budget in mind
  2. Sets a budget and item types

  3. Buy new or secondhand?

    New Shows department store and off-price options, focusing on sales
    Secondhand Shows thrift and resale listings
  4. Browses, compares and saves items

  5. Is the plan over budget?

    Yes Suggests cheaper alternatives, or lower-priority items to wait on
    No Goes straight to the list
  6. A budgeted shopping list with links to buy

03 · Find the right fit

As a young professional who struggles to find clothes that fit, I want to save my measurements and filter for fit and length, so that I can shop online with confidence and avoid returns or clothes that don’t fit.

Problem
Professional clothes that fit well are hard to find and shop for, especially for people whose fit needs standard sizing doesn’t cover, like extended lengths.
Why it matters
Poor fit undermines the polished look people want at work, and can reflect poorly on them and the company.
Scenario
Maya is shopping online for work pants with a 34″ inseam. Most options come in standard lengths, and she can’t tell whether they’ll fit.

Steps

  1. Enter measurements or preferred fit The app saves a fit profile.
  2. Search for pants Results show only items that come in her length or fit.
  3. Read fit notes and reviews from similar body types Each item shows fit feedback.
  4. Pick an item and size The app confirms the recommended size and the return policy.

Flow

  1. Searches for a specific item, like pants

  2. Has a saved fit profile?

    Yes Carries on
    No Prompted to create one
  3. Filters by length and fit

  4. Are there any matches?

    Yes Shows fit reviews
    No Suggests retailers with extended sizing, or tailoring tips
  5. Item added to the cart or wardrobe board, with a recommended size
06

Site map

I organized the app around five areas reachable from the home page. Explore leads to filters and individual items, and saved clothes feed the outfit generator. Items and outfits each get a score, the shopping list tracks spending against your budget, and buying happens on the retailer’s own site.

07

Wireframes

I sketched the main screens by hand to work out the layout before moving into Figma. Each one follows a page from the site map, with the same four-tab bar along the bottom.
